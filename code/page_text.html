On a clear, moonless night far from any town, the Milky Way crosses the sky as a pale river of light. Most people alive today will never see it from their own doorstep. The light we spill upwards from streets, buildings and car parks scatters off air and dust and comes back down as a glow that drowns the faint stars. Astronomers call it sky glow, and it can be mapped.

I built my own map of it. Satellites photograph the Earth at night; the VIIRS instrument measures how much light leaves every patch of ground. I took those measurements for the whole world in 2023, spread each patch's light across the sky around it with a simple rule for how glow fades with distance, and tuned that rule against {{model.n_fit|int}} readings that Globe at Night volunteers took with hand-held sky meters. Then I asked who lives under each kind of sky. By this model, {{world.pop_mw|pct}} of the world's people, about {{world.people_mw_bn|f1}} billion, live where the glow hides the Milky Way. In the United Kingdom it is {{uk.pop_mw|pct}}.

02 · The worldWho can still see the Milky Way?

The thresholds come from the most careful study of the problem, the World Atlas of Artificial Night Sky Brightness by Fabio Falchi and colleagues (2016). They take the natural night sky as 174 µcd/m² (22.0 magnitudes per square arcsecond) and say the Milky Way is hidden once artificial glow passes 688 µcd/m², about four times the natural sky. I use the same lines, so my numbers can sit beside theirs.

My figure is higher than the atlas's, and I think the honest reading is that the truth lies somewhere between the two. Mine uses lights from 2023 rather than 2014, a population grid that puts people only where there are buildings, and sky meter readings that volunteers mostly take in the evening, when more lights are on than at the satellite's 1:30 am pass. The atlas uses a full model of how light scatters in the atmosphere, which mine does not. But the two agree closely on which countries are worst and best: across {{falchi.compare.n|int}} countries the rank correlation is {{falchi.compare.spearman|f2}}. On average mine is {{falchi.mean_diff_pts|int}} percentage points higher. Ukraine is one of the few big countries where my 2023 figure is well below the atlas's ({{country.UKR.pop_mw|pct}} against {{country.UKR.falchi_pop_mw|pct}}), as you would expect after the wartime blackouts.

06 · ChangeIs the UK getting darker?

From space, yes. The total light VIIRS recorded from Great Britain fell by {{change.gbr_pct|abs1}}% between 2015 and 2023, while Ireland's rose by {{change.irl_pct|f1}}%. Run with 2015 lights, the model puts {{change.pop_mw_2015|pct}} of UK residents under a Milky Way hiding sky, against {{change.pop_mw_2023|pct}} with 2023 lights, and {{change.land_dimmer|pct}} of UK land has a sky at least 5% dimmer, against {{change.land_brighter|pct}} brighter. The biggest falls were in Tyne and Wear ({{uk_light_fall.0.pct|abs1}}%), Inverclyde and Northumberland. Lights rose in only {{uk_areas_light_up|int}} of the {{uk_areas_n|int}} areas, most of all in the Western Isles (+{{uk_light_rise.0.pct|f1}}%) and Cornwall (+{{uk_light_rise.1.pct|f1}}%).

But I would not trust that from the ground. Over these years councils replaced orange sodium street lamps with white LEDs. LEDs are often dimmer and better aimed, which helps. But they give out much of their light at blue wavelengths, which the VIIRS sensor barely sees and which scatters more in the air. A street can look darker from space and brighter to the eye. Volunteers counting stars for Globe at Night found the opposite of the satellite: across the world, the stars they could see faded fast between 2011 and 2022, equivalent to the sky brightening by about 10% a year (Kyba and colleagues, 2023). The fall in the UK's lights from space is real, but it is probably not a fall of the same size in sky glow.

How I did this

Lights. The Earth Observation Group at the Colorado School of Mines makes yearly cloud-free averages of what the VIIRS Day/Night Band sees at about 1:30 am (VNL version 2), with gas flares and volcanoes removed. WorldPop republishes them per country at 3 arc seconds (about 90 m) under an open licence. I read all {{meta.world_files|int}} country files for 2023 in a web browser and averaged them to a world grid of 2.5 arc minutes (about 4.6 km), and the UK, Ireland and Isle of Man files for every year from 2015 to 2023 to a 30 arc second grid (about 0.9 km by 0.5 km).

Glow. Each cell's light (radiance times lit area) is spread to every place within {{model.r_max|int}} km with weight (d² + 1)−α/2, d in km, and the sky at a place is k times the total. Lights outside the UK reach the UK grid from the world grid. This is the old rule of thumb that sky glow falls off with a power of distance (Walker's law has 2.5), not a full radiative transfer model: it ignores hills, haze, height and the colour and angle of lamps.

Tuning and checking. I took Globe at Night Sky Quality Meter readings from 2019 to 2025, made under a clear sky after 8 pm, and chose α and k to match the {{model.n18|int}} readings darker than {{model.fit_min|f1}} magnitudes (the 4.6 km grid cannot resolve bright city-centre streets) with least absolute error. α = {{model.alpha|f1}} worked best. Tested on regions left out of the fit (five folds of 10° blocks), the model misses a typical reading by {{model.cv18_mae|f2}} magnitudes, with correlation {{model.cv18_r|f2}}. On the {{model.uk_fine.n|int}} readings inside the UK the 1 km model misses by {{model.uk_fine.mae|f2}}. It underestimates the brightest city centres badly, which is why city figures here are shown as "never truly dark" rather than with precise values.

Model against measurement
A random 1,500 of the {{model.n_fit|int}} Globe at Night readings, 2019 to 2025, against the model at the same place. Dashed line: perfect agreement.

People. WorldPop's 2023 population grid (R2025A, constrained to built-up areas, 30 arc seconds), summed to each model grid. On the coarse world grid people share their cell's average sky, while in reality they cluster under the brightest part of it, so world figures for people may be too low; on the same UK population the coarse grid gives {{uk.coarse_pop_mw|pct}} under a Milky Way hiding sky, the 1 km grid {{uk.pop_mw|pct}}.

Limits. This is a model. It is tuned to readings taken mostly in North America and Europe, by volunteers, in the evening; it says nothing about clouds, the Moon or the season. The finest distinctions near natural darkness (the 1% and 8% lines) are within the noise of the sky meters, so I lean on the Milky Way line. Kosovo has no light file of its own in the WorldPop set and is left out of country figures. I did not use the Falchi atlas grid itself: it is published under a non-commercial licence, with a note in the download that forbids further distribution, so I quote only the figures printed in their paper. I could not find an open licence for CPRE's Night Blight maps of England, so they are not used either.
